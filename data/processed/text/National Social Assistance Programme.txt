EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP), launched in August 1995, was designed to provide

a safety net for the most disadvantaged and vulnerable sections of Indian society. Currently, NSAP has

five major schemes under its ambit: Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme (IGNOAPS), Indira

Gandhi National Widow Pension Scheme (IGNWPS), Indira Gandhi National Disability Pension Scheme

(IGNDPS), National Family Benefit Scheme (NFBS), and the Annapurna Scheme. Over the years, NSAP

has evolved into a key pillar of India’s social protection architecture, with substantial financial outlays

aimed at ensuring basic income security for the poorest households. However, the Comptroller and

Auditor General (CAG) of India’s 2023 performance audit revealed wide state-level disparities and

several systemic gaps in planning, financial management, and monitoring. The audit found instances

of both exclusions where eligible beneficiaries were left out and inclusion errors where ineligible

persons received benefits due to weaknesses in verification, data maintenance, and authentication

systems. Additionally, irregularities in payment schedules and partial non-compliance with Direct

Benefit Transfer (DBT) mechanisms were noted, prompting a series of corrective recommendations

from the CAG. Against this backdrop, a regular independent evaluation of NSAP was critical to gauge

its effectiveness, assess progress toward its objectives, and identify measures to enhance delivery and

accountability. In this context, the Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD) commissioned Academy of

Management Studies (AMS) to undertake an Impact Assessment and Evaluation of NSAP to assess the

programme’s achievements and recommend actionable strategies for its strengthening and

continuation.

The broad objectives of the study were to:

 ➢ Analyze the achievements of NSAP objectives,

 ➢ Assess scheme-level implementation, and

 ➢ Recommend measures to enhance scheme delivery and accountability.

The study employed a mixed-methods approach across ten sample states representing different

pension categories, such as Haryana, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh (high), Gujarat, Jammu &

Kashmir, Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh (moderate), and Assam, Chhattisgarh and Bihar (low). Data

collection combined structured surveys with 6,000 beneficiaries across 600 Gram Panchayats (GPs),

semi-structured interviews with local elected representatives, focus group discussions with

community members, and in-depth interviews with district and state level officials to capture both

implementation realities and administrative perspectives.

Key findings of the study, mapped against the broad objectives, have been depicted below –

I. Analysis of the achievements of the objectives of NSAP

Reducing financial dependency - About 75.1% of beneficiaries reported that the pension has

somewhat reduced their dependence on other earning members of the household, while 16.5% said

it has significantly lowered their financial dependency. These findings highlight that even modest

financial support under NSAP enhances beneficiaries’ financial autonomy and household stability. In

states such as Andhra Pradesh and Haryana, where higher state top-ups ranging from ₹1,000 to ₹3,000

per month are provided, the scheme has led to notable improvements in beneficiary satisfaction.

Improvement in Socio-economic status- NSAP pensions have contributed to increased household

consumption, improved healthcare access, and enhanced food adequacy, as the study finds that 81-

84% of beneficiaries spent the pension amount for food and medicine. A majority of beneficiaries

reported better access to food (63%), an increased capacity to purchase daily commodities (68%),

and improved access to medical care (65%). The scheme has also reduced the need to borrow for

subsistence among half of the respondents. States such as Jammu & Kashmir, Telangana, and Tamil

Nadu recorded particularly strong outcomes, with over 70-90% of beneficiaries reporting

improvement in these areas.

Ensuring Dignity, Social Security and Safety- Across states, around 67-69% of beneficiaries reported

an improvement in their social acceptance, treatment within the family, and self-esteem, reflecting

how the pension contributes to restoring self-worth and social security. States such as Jammu &

Kashmir, and Telangana showed widespread positive changes, indicating strong social recognition and

empowerment among beneficiaries. However, 13.3% of beneficiaries reported being able to save any

portion of their pension, indicating that while the scheme supports basic sustenance, the amount

remains largely inadequate.

II. Scheme level Analysis

Beneficiary Awareness - Awareness level about NSAP schemes is moderate but uneven across states.

31.6% of beneficiaries were aware of the scheme implementing department, and only about half

knew about the central and state contribution components. Awareness levels were high in Andhra

Pradesh, Telangana, and Tamil Nadu, where active community engagement and outreach through

local institutions and digital campaigns have helped strengthen information flow. In contrast, limited

awareness was observed in states like Bihar, Chhattisgarh, and Gujarat.

Implementation framework and eligibility- The NSAP framework is found to be effectively

implemented across most states, with clear administrative responsibilities at state, district, and Gram

Panchayat levels. However, the study observed inconsistencies in beneficiary identification due to

reliance on outdated Below Poverty Line (BPL) lists. Almost all the states covered in this study follows

the BPL criteria. Though a very few instances of exclusion of eligible and inclusion of ineligible

beneficiaries were reported, states such as Haryana, which use a Family ID database for automatic

Disbursement, DBT and Beneficiary Convenience - The implementation of Direct Benefit Transfer

(DBT) across all states (except Andhra Pradesh) has improved transparency and accountability in

Satisfaction and Adequacy - 73% of the beneficiaries surveyed were satisfied with the overall

process of the pension scheme. However, the majority also reported that the pension amount is

insufficient to meet essential needs such as food, healthcare, and utilities. 89% of the beneficiaries

believed that the government should contribute more funds to increase the pension amount and

improve related services. Beneficiaries in states with higher state top-ups (Andhra Pradesh,

Telangana, Tamil Nadu) showed significantly greater satisfaction and improved well-being compared

to those in low-top-up states (Assam, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh).

Social Audit- The study also found that social audits are not conducted regularly across many states,

limiting community oversight and accountability. Overall, 53.5% of gram panchayat and ward officials

confirmed that a social audit had been conducted, while 30.7% reported none, and 15.8% were

unaware. The highest incidence of social audits was recorded in Assam (86.7%), Uttar Pradesh (80%),

states.

Grievance Redressal- 377 out of 600 panchayat and ward officials reported having a grievance

Gram Sabha meetings emerged as the most common platform for raising grievances (reported by

80.1% of officials), with delays in pension disbursement being the most common concern among

beneficiaries.

Best Practices- In Jammu & Kashmir, awareness about the scheme is widely promoted through print,

electronic, and community-based platforms such as Anganwadi and ASHA workers. Haryana has

introduced a transparent Family ID system that automatically updates beneficiary information with

minimum interference form gram panchayat and ward officials, ensuring fair and efficient delivery.

III. Recommendations & Way Forward

Justification for the continuation of the scheme: Despite India’s rapid economic growth, the central

pension component has remained unchanged since 2012, leading to significant decline in its real value

due to inflation and rising living costs. Strengthening and expanding NSAP is therefore critical to ensuring

dignity and social security for vulnerable citizens. Moreover, aligning pension levels with inflation, poverty

benchmarks, and international norms will make the scheme more adequate and equitable.

Recommendations on improving the scheme implementation: The Below Poverty Line (BPL) criteria

should be replaced with a more reliable and integrated beneficiary identification system based on

verified databases such as Aadhaar, social registries, or state Family ID systems to ensure accurate

targeting and reduce duplication. The criteria and verification process may be left to the discretion

of individual states. To ensure last-mile efficiency, beneficiary facilitation centres at village or ward

levels could be established to assist the elderly, widows, and persons with disabilities in registration,

documentation, and grievance redressal. The pension amount should be suitably revised by

introducing a National Floor Pension, linked to the Consumer Price Index (CPI) and periodically

adjusted to reflect the cost of living, thereby ensuring a decent standard of life. Mechanisms for

doorstep pension delivery, as practiced in Haryana and Andhra Pradesh, should be adopted by other

states to support beneficiaries with mobility challenges. Further, convergence with other welfare

schemes such as PMAY, NFSA, health insurance, and other livelihood programmes should be

established to enhance social and economic resilience. The grievance redressal system should be

made more accessible through toll-free helplines, Panchayat-level complaint cells, and escalation

protocols integrated with the NSAP-PPS portal. Regular social audits, data-driven monitoring

dashboards, and periodic third-party evaluations should be institutionalized to ensure transparency,

accountability, and continuous improvement in scheme delivery.

In conclusion, the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) continues to play a significant role in

India’s social protection framework, contributing to poverty reduction and providing social security to

the elderly, widows, and persons with disabilities. However, the study highlights the need to

strengthen key areas such as beneficiary identification, timely disbursement of pensions, and proper

maintenance of records to improve efficiency and transparency. Enhancing convergence with state

schemes, ensuring regular indexation of pension amounts to inflation, and adopting a robust

monitoring and grievance redressal system will be crucial to achieving the scheme’s goal of ensuring

a life of dignity and security for all vulnerable citizens.

 Context – Background Study

1.1 INTRODUCTION

The National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) derives its mandate from Article 41 of the
Constitution, as part of the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)1, which states that “the State

shall, within the limits of its economic capacity and development, make effective provision for securing

the right to…public assistance in case of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, and in

other cases of undeserved want.” Although not enforceable in the court of law as a Fundamental
Right, Prabhat Patnaik (2018)2 argues that “adequate pension to the elderly is an economic right”,

which is “not codified as such in the Constitution”, but “implicit and as fundamental as the explicitly-

codified fundamental rights”. To fulfil the constitutional obligation, Government of India introduced

NSAP in 1995 as a welfare programme aimed at providing basic financial support to the destitutes and

BPL households. The scheme is being implemented by the Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD). It

includes 3 pension schemes targeting old aged (above 60 years), widows, and disabled persons living

below poverty line. In addition, a family benefit scheme offering one-time assistance to the bereaved

family in the event of death of the breadwinner is also included under the ambit of NSAP.

As per the CAG report of 2023, during 2017-21, Government of India allocated ₹ 34,432 crore for

NSAP. In addition to this ₹ 1,09,573 crore was allocated by States and UTs to offer additional

assistance for pension and for covering beneficiaries that were left out from the coverage of

NSAP. A total of 2.83 crore beneficiaries were covered annually through funds allocated by central

government, and additional 1.82 crore beneficiaries were covered through States/UTs funds. The

coverage of the scheme spans across both, urban and rural areas across all states and UTs in the

country. The beneficiaries for the scheme are identified on the basis of BPL list prepared by the

states and UTs as per guidelines issued by the MoRD for rural areas and by the Ministry of Urban

Housing & Poverty Alleviation for urban areas. In addition, the states also have the power to

identify additional eligible beneficiaries in case they identify cases where the person’s name is not

included in the BPL list, but he or she satisfies the given eligibility criteria.

1.1.1 Components of NSAP

The National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) comprises five major sub-schemes aimed at providing

financial and food security support to vulnerable sections of the population. These include:

➢ Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme (IGNOAPS): Offers financial assistance of ₹200

 per month to individuals aged 60–79 years and ₹500 per month to those aged 80 and above.

 Currently, more than 221 lakh beneficiaries are covered.

➢ Indira Gandhi National Widow Pension Scheme (IGNWPS): Provides ₹300 per month to widows

 aged 40–79 years and ₹500 to those 80 and above. Currently, more than 67 lakh

 beneficiaries are covered.

➢ Indira Gandhi National Disability Pension Scheme (IGNDPS): Financial assistance of ₹300 per month

 to individuals with severe or multiple disabilities aged 18–79 and ₹500 to those aged 80

 years and above. Currently, more than 8.8 lakh beneficiaries are covered.

➢ National Family Benefit Scheme (NFBS): Grants one-time financial assistance of ₹20,000 to

 bereaved families upon the death of the primary breadwinner aged between 18 and 59

 years. The upper limit of households is 3.58 lakh.

➢ Annapurna Scheme: Provided 10 kg of food grains per month free of cost to those senior

 citizens who, though eligible, were not receiving old age pension. It was a demand-based

 scheme with an upper limit of 8.32 lakh beneficiaries.

However, there has been no uptake of the scheme since 2019. This may be attributed to the fact

that most households now receive food grains under the National Food Security Act (NFSA),

which, while not part of NSAP, effectively addresses their food security needs.

Initially launched as a centrally sponsored scheme, NSAP underwent several changes, including

the transfer of the National Maternity Benefit Scheme to the Department of Family Welfare, the

introduction of Annapurna in 2000, and its devolution to state plans in 2002–03. In 2007, coverage

was expanded to all BPL persons, and in 2009, pensions for widows and persons with disabilities

were introduced. The scheme specifically targets Below Poverty Line (BPL) individuals and
continues to evolve to address the needs of India’s most vulnerable populations.3

1.1.2 Funding & Coverage

The National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) has undergone significant changes in its funding

and coverage since its inception. Initially transferred to the State Plan in 2002, funds were routed

through the Ministry of Finance for States and the Ministry of Home Affairs for Union Territories until

2013–14. However, from 1st April 2014, NSAP was converted into a Centrally Sponsored Scheme

(CSS), and since then, the Ministry of Rural Development has been directly releasing funds to States

and UTs. Notably, in December 2016, NSAP was designated as a “Core of the Core” scheme by NITI

Aayog. Currently, the allocation of central assistance is based on either the State Cap or the digitized

number of beneficiaries on the NSAP-Pension Payment System (PPS), whichever is lower. The State

Cap, i.e., the number of beneficiaries under each scheme for each State/UT is derived using the 2001

Census population and the poverty ratio as estimated by the erstwhile Planning Commission.

Currently, NSAP provides pensions to approximately 3 crores Below Poverty Line (BPL) beneficiaries,

and States determine the estimated number of beneficiaries for the following year based on reports

from the previous year. In addition to the central support, States supplement the pension amount,

ranging from ₹50 to ₹5700 per month, and extend coverage to additional beneficiaries using their

own resources. This has enabled around 5.87 crore additional pensioners to be covered under

various State-run schemes, bringing the total number of beneficiaries receiving pension support

under NSAP and State schemes to nearly 9 crores. The three pension schemes namely, IGNOAPS,

IGNDPS and IGNWPS are 100% saturated. However, National Family Benefit Scheme (NFBS) is

demand based. To improve transparency and ensure faster transactions, the NSAP pension
schemes were brought under the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) schemes in December 2014. 4Many

state governments have their own social pension schemes for vulnerable groups. Hence, there

remains significant variation across States in eligibility criteria, assistance norms, and benefit

amounts, reflecting diverse policy approaches to social assistance.

Budget Allocation

The budget allocation for the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) has remained largely

stagnant between FY 2021-22 and FY 2024-25, reflecting limited fiscal expansion in social pensions.

Under IGNOAPS, allocations stabilized around ₹6,600–₹7,000 crore in subsequent years, constituting

nearly 70% of the total NSAP budget.

Similar stability is observed under IGNWPS and IGNDPS, with allocations around ₹2,000 crore and

₹290 crore, respectively, indicating no major enhancement in benefit size or coverage. In contrast, the

National Family Benefit Scheme (NFBS) shows fluctuating allocations, with a notable reduction in

2024-25 (RE) to ₹300 crore, suggesting lower uptake or delayed disbursal, while the Annapurna

Scheme has become almost nominal at ₹10 crore, signaling its near phase-out.

1.2 RATIONALE OF THE STUDY

NSAP has made significant financial outlays, and is vital for strengthening the social protection

system in the country. In 2023, an All-India performance audit of the NSAP was undertaken by CAG,

which reflected that although centrally financed, the NSAP’s implementation and the amount

of benefits, as well as its impact, varies from state to state. The report revealed several

irregularities and lapses in the planning, financial management, implementation, and

monitoring of the welfare scheme NSAP. It highlighted that as the scheme is being implemented

in demand-driven mode, there are chances that many of the eligible beneficiaries are left-out of the

 5

--- PAGE 19 ---
coverage of scheme. On the other hand, it was also noted that many of the ineligible

beneficiaries were also drawing benefits from the scheme owing to deficiencies in the

verification mechanism, maintenance of requisite data and authentication systems. The

evaluation also highlighted lapses in payment mechanism including untimely and irregular

payments as well as the system being non-DBT compliant in some states. Based on their observations,

the CAG offered multiple recommendations to improve the performance of the scheme.

Regular independent evaluation of the scheme at this juncture therefore assumes great importance

to assess the status of scheme performance and identify the key enablers and barriers

influencing scheme implementation. It is also important from the perspective of vitality of

the scheme to help meet the sustainable development goals which is a prime agenda for the

Government. In view of the aforementioned factors, and with the objective of strengthening the

monitoring and evaluation systems for scheme implementation, AMS (Academy of Management

Studies) was commissioned by the Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD) through its Department of Rural

Development (DoRD) to conduct the “Impact Assessment & Evaluation of NSAP”.

3.1 ACHIEVEMENTS OF THE SCHEME

Launched in 1995, the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) has emerged as one of India’s

largest and longest-running social protection frameworks, fulfilling the constitutional vision under

Article 41 to support citizens facing old age, widowhood, disability, or the loss of a breadwinner. Over

nearly three decades, NSAP has grown into a nationwide safety net covering nearly 3 crore

beneficiaries. Its expansion, institutionalization, and direct impact on improving the quality of life

among the poorest households make NSAP one of the most significant pillars of India’s social

protection ecosystem.

 1. Strengthening Household Security and Reducing
 Financial Vulnerability
 • 75.1% of beneficiaries reported that the pension
 somewhat reduced their dependence on other
 earning members in their household; 16.5% said
 it significantly reduced dependence.

 • States with higher top-ups like Andhra Pradesh,
 Telangana, and Haryana, showed stronger
 improvements in household stability and
 income security, demonstrating the stabilizing
 role of NSAP in low-income households.

 • The scheme provides predictable, regular
 support for elderly persons, widows, and
 persons with disabilities who are traditionally
 excluded from the labour market.

 2. Improvements in Socio-Economic Well-Being
 • 81-84% beneficiaries use pensions primarily for food and medicine.

 • 63% reported improved food access, 68% improved ability to purchase personal essentials,
 and 65% improved medical access.

 • Nearly half the respondents borrow less for subsistence, indicating reduced distress
 borrowing and better resilience.

• Strongest improvements recorded in Jammu & Kashmir, Telangana, and Tamil Nadu, where

 3. Enhancing Dignity, Social Acceptance, and Self-Worth

 • 67-68% beneficiaries reported improved treatment and respect within their families and
 communities.

 • Elderly persons, widows, and persons with disabilities noted greater social acceptance,

3.4.2 Identification of Beneficiaries

Beneficiary identification processes vary significantly across states, reflecting differences in local

implementation practices. Overall, Gram Sabha meetings (36.3%) and panchayat officials (33.5%) were

the most common means of identifying beneficiaries, indicating the crucial role of local governance

institutions in the identification and selection process. States like Bihar (66.5%), Chhattisgarh (55.5%),

outreach methods. Some beneficiaries were also identified through ‘other’ local functionaries, such as

strengthen inclusive and participatory mechanisms, particularly in states where self-identification

dominates or community outreach is limited.

3.4.7 Implementation of NSAP: As per Key Stakeholders

Discussions and interviews with state and district officials were conducted to understand the

implementation of the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) in detail. The processes,

especially those related to beneficiary identification, verification, and fund disbursement vary across

states. Some state-specific processes have been provided below:

Gujarat

Gujarat follows a fully digital, portal-based application and verification system for NSAP pensions.

Application Process: Beneficiaries apply either through Digital Gujarat Portal (state platform) or

UMANG App (national platform)

Steps Involved:

 ➢ Online registration on Digital Gujarat/UMANG.

 ➢ Selection of the relevant NSAP pension scheme (IGNOAPS/IGNWPS/IGNDPS).

 ➢ Uploading mandatory documents (age proof, BPL/income proof, Aadhaar, bank details).

 ➢ Forwarding of the application to verification authorities.

 ➢ Verification by district or block officials and approval by the Social Defence Department.

 ➢ Integration with DBT for regular pension disbursement.

--- PAGE 49 ---
Further discussions with state officials revealed several challenges associated with receiving

applications through the UMANG app. Officials noted that the app allows only data entry, with no

option for applicants to upload supporting documents, making it difficult to verify eligibility. As a

result, officials are required to re-enter these applications into the state portal, where Aadhaar

authentication and document uploads take place. This duplication of effort increases workload, causes

delays, and reduces the overall efficiency of the application process.

Additionally, the UMANG app can be accessed only through mobile phones and not via laptops or

desktops, which creates operational constraints for staff who typically work on computer-based

systems. Officials also highlighted that many elderly and persons with disabilities are unable to use

mobile applications, limiting their ability to apply through UMANG and reducing its effectiveness as

an inclusive service delivery platform.

Disbursement: The state follows a structured fund flow mechanism for NSAP pensions. Funds from

the Central Government are routed to the State Treasury through the Single Nodal Agency (SNA).

The State oversee the actual disbursement of pensions to beneficiaries’ bank accounts via DBT.

Assam

In Assam, the identification of NSAP beneficiaries follows a community-based, application-driven

process led by the Panchayat and Rural Development Department. A key eligibility requirement is

that applicants must belong to a Below Poverty Line (BPL) household.

Application Process: Local officials and volunteers collect applications from eligible households at

the village level. Applications are verified to confirm age, BPL status, and other required criteria.

Verified beneficiary lists are presented in the Gram Sabha for discussion and community validation.

The approved list is then forwarded to the designated sanctioning authority for processing.

Disbursement: NSAP payments in Assam are largely made through Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT).

Monthly pensions are deposited directly into beneficiaries’ bank or post office accounts, reflecting

a strong preference for digital and transparent payments.

Tamil Nadu

Tamil Nadu follows a hybrid application system, combining e-Seva centres with rigorous field

verification by revenue officials.

Application Process: Individuals unfamiliar with the process typically visit the Taluk Office, where they

are guided to the e-Seva Centre. After submission, the application is sent to the Village Administrative

Officer (VAO) for field verification. The VAO forwards verified applications to the Revenue Inspector (RI),

who reviews both the VAO report and field findings. The RI submits the application to the Tahsildar

through district website, who gives final approval. Final sanction depends on the availability of funds

 36

--- PAGE 50 ---
depending on the number of beneficiaries fixed for the CRA (treasury authority). Most applications are

now submitted online through e-Seva centres, improving accessibility. Applicants receive SMS

notifications on their registered mobile numbers for tracking application status. Field verification

continues to be carried out by VAOs and RIs to maintain accuracy.

Disbursement: DBT is the primary mode of disbursement, with payments made between the 5th
and 6th of every month. About 94% of payments are DBT, and the remaining 6-8% are through

electronic money orders (EMO). No payments are made in cash; all transfers are routed through

formal banking or postal systems.

Telangana

In Telangana, NSAP schemes are implemented in coordination with Panchayat Secretaries, Mandal-

level staff, and the District Rural Development Agency.

Application Process: Applications are collected at the Panchayat level, verified at the Mandal level,

and approved at the district level.

• Gram Panchayat: Receives applications and assists citizens with documentation

• Mandal Development Office: Verifies and forwards applications.

• District Rural Development Agency and District Collector: Provides the final approval.

• Post Office: Handles DBT-based payments.

Village Secretaries, SHG Leaders, and GP assist in awareness generation. Mandal-level staff conduct

field verification of documents including ration cards, Aadhaar, and death certificates.

Disbursement: Funds are directly transferred from the State Finance Department to the District

Treasury. After Aadhaar seeding, funds are credited to beneficiaries’ bank accounts through

DBT/APB systems. Many elderly and disabled beneficiaries face difficulty in biometric

authentication or operating bank accounts, which makes DBT transactions difficult. In such cases,

the bank staff or field functionaries withdraw the DBT amount and disburse it in cash after proper

verification.

Andhra Pradesh

Andhra Pradesh follows a highly structured, technology-enabled system through the Gram/Ward

Secretariat framework.

Application Process: Beneficiary identification and selection is led by Welfare Assistants at the

Gram/Ward Secretariat level. The entire process, including scrutiny and sanction of eligible

beneficiaries, are supervised and approved by the Mandal Parishad Development Officers (MPDOs)

in rural areas and the Municipal Commissioners (MCs) in urban areas, serving as the sanctioning

authorities at the Mandal / Municipality level. The sanctioning authority for pensions at the Block

Level is the Mandal Parishad Development Officer (MPDO), who processes the sanctions through

Welfare Assistants after verification.

The verification of applications is carried out in a systematic manner to ensure transparency and

accuracy in beneficiary selection. The procedure generally involves the following steps:

• Field Verification: Welfare Assistants and Secretariat staff conduct field visits to the applicant’s

 residence to verify the details submitted in the application. During these visits, socio-economic

 conditions and eligibility criteria are cross-checked.

• Record Verification: Relevant records such as Aadhaar card, ration card, death certificate (in

 case of NFBS), income certificate, and other supporting documents are verified to confirm

 eligibility.

• Cross-Checking at Secretariat Level: Verified applications are reviewed at the Secretariat level

 by the concerned functionaries and approved by the Welfare and Revenue officials.

• Supervisory Verification: MPDOs, Tahsildars, and other supervisory officers conduct random

 checks and field inspections to ensure accuracy and prevent any irregularities.

• Frequency of Verification: Field verification is done for every new application, and periodic

 reviews are conducted during monthly meetings or whenever special verification drives are

 instructed by higher authorities.

This multi-layered verification system ensures transparency, accountability, and accurate targeting

of eligible beneficiaries.

Disbursement: Funds reach Secretariat-level accounts by the 29th or 30th each month who then

transfer it to the District Secretariat bank accounts. The respective Panchayat Secretaries or Welfare

Secretaries withdraw the funds and disburse them to the concerned pension disbursement

functionaries by the 30th of every month. On the 1st of each month, all disbursement functionaries

distribute pensions door-to-door using the biometric system, ensuring timely and accurate delivery

to beneficiaries.

Haryana

Application Process: Haryana implements old-age, disability, and widow pensions through Pro-Active

Pension Mode, enabled by the state’s digital Family ID and CRID systems. Instead of relying on the

traditional Below Poverty Line (BPL) list, the state automatically identifies eligible individuals such as

senior citizens, persons with disabilities, widows, widowers, unmarried persons above a specified age,

and destitute individuals using demographic and socio-economic details like age, income, Aadhaar,

mobile number, and residence history verified by the CRID Department. Once an individual meets the

eligibility criteria, their information becomes available on the Pro-Active platform and is shared with

the Social Justice, Empowerment, Welfare of SC & BC and Antyodaya Department (SEWA

Department). Officials from the District Social Welfare Department then contact or visit beneficiaries

to collect applications, obtain consent, verify documents such as Aadhaar and Family ID, capture

photographs, and generate a Pension Identification Number, ensuring that even those who do not

apply themselves are covered. Beneficiaries also have the option to apply online from anywhere, after

which district officials call them to the block or district office for verification before forwarding the

application for processing. Approvals are granted at the district level, and pensions are subsequently

sanctioned and credited directly to the beneficiary’s bank account.

Disbursement: Funds are transferred from the Centre to the State through the Finance Department,

after which the State disburses pensions to beneficiaries every month through the PFMS (DBT) mode.

Aadhaar seeding has been completed for all beneficiaries.

Uttar Pradesh

Application Process: Beneficiaries apply online either from home or through Common Service Centres

(CSCs) and cyber cafés. Once the online application is submitted on the portal, verification is

conducted in block level as well as village-level officials. Key documents such as income certificates,

age proof, disability certificates, bank passbooks, and Aadhaar are verified during this stage. Gram

Panchayats play a central role in identifying and selecting eligible persons, with ward members,

panchayat assistants, and village development officers actively involved in recommending

beneficiaries. Eligibility verification of online applications is carried out at the district level by the

Tehsil/Block or District Social Welfare Officer, after which approval is granted. In addition to the

regular application system, the state also provides pensions to eligible elderly persons under the

National Old Age Pension Scheme through the “One Family One ID” Family ID system, which

automatically identifies eligible beneficiaries. All payments are made through online transfer directly

to the beneficiary’s bank account.

Disbursement: Under NSAP, funds are transferred to beneficiaries’ Aadhaar-linked bank accounts

through the PFMS portal. Mobile numbers of beneficiaries are linked to their bank accounts. Pension

amounts are disbursed quarterly, i.e., once every three months.

Chhattisgarh

Application Process: In Chhattisgarh, the pension process begins with identifying beneficiaries at the

village level, where applications are reviewed and approved during Gram Sabha meetings. Self-help

groups (SHGs), ward members, and panchayat representatives play an active role in the identification

process. After the Gram Sabha recommends beneficiaries, the Gram Panchayat forwards applications

to the Janpad Panchayat within 7 days, along with their recommendations. At the Janpad Panchayat

or municipality level, a committee reviews the applications and sanctions or rejects them within 60

days. Verification of applicants, particularly to confirm whether they belong to the BPL category is

carried out by Panchayat Secretaries. Once approved, pensions are disbursed to the beneficiaries

through Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT).

Disbursement: The State Nodal Agency (Directorate of Social Welfare) draws the funds from the

treasury and transfers them to the State Nodal Account (SNA) through PFMS. Pension amounts are

disbursed monthly to beneficiaries via DBT from the SNA using the NSAP-PPS and PFMS platforms.

Aadhaar-based payments are followed, and both Aadhaar seeding and mobile number linkage are

completed for DBT.

Bihar

Application Process: In Bihar, beneficiaries apply for pensions through the RTPS counter, after which

entries are made into the SSPMIS portal. Applications are processed at multiple levels starting from

the block, where they are initially received, and moving upward for verification. The verification

process involves the Social Welfare Area Supervisor, Village Development authorities, the Block

Development Officer (BDO), the Sub-Divisional Officer (SDO), and the District Social Welfare Officer.

The scheme requires detailed documentation, including a death certificate of spouse for widow

pensions, income certificates from a competent authority, a ration card and age proof. Aadhaar, voter

ID, BPL cards, photographs, and bank account details are used to verify the identity of applicants. Final

approval is granted by a District-Level Committee, after which pensions are released at the district

level and sent to the beneficiary's account through DBT.

Disbursement: Pensions are disbursed monthly through DBT. From the State, funds are disbursed

through PFMS under the DBT system. Aadhaar seeding and authentication are completed for all

beneficiaries. Field verification is carried out in cases of failed transactions.

Jammu & Kashmir

Application process: Beneficiaries are identified by the Department of Social Welfare through village

Anganwadi workers, who conduct quarterly and annual surveys to collect accurate household

information such as gender, age, caste, income, and other required details. Most beneficiaries submit

their application forms either directly to the District Social Welfare Officer (DSWO) or through nearby

CSC centres. All applications are processed through the Jansugam portal.

Disbursement: Pension payments are disbursed monthly through Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) from

the Director, Finance, Department of Social Welfare directly to the beneficiary’s bank account

3.4.8.2 Special arrangements for effective last-mile delivery

To strengthen last-mile delivery of pensions, several states have adopted measures to ensure that

beneficiaries receive support in a timely and accessible manner:

Andhra Pradesh:

Pensions are disbursed at centrally located and easily accessible points in the village between the

1st and 3rd of every month. For elderly or physically challenged beneficiaries who are unable to

travel, designated officials from the Panchayat Bhawan Secretariat deliver the pension amount

directly to their homes.

Haryana:

The Social Welfare Department has partnered with banks to provide doorstep services. If a

beneficiary is unable to visit the bank due to health or mobility constraints, they can inform the bank,

following which bank officials complete the required formalities and arrange for pension delivery at

the beneficiary’s residence.

Tamil Nadu:

Some beneficiaries receive pension through the post office system, with postmen providing

doorstep delivery where needed. In certain areas, mobile ATM facilities are also available. Some

NGOs support differently-abled beneficiaries, and banking correspondents also assist in delivering

pensions. Postmen and BCs visit the homes of beneficiaries who are unable to travel.

Jammu & Kashmir:

 In areas where bank branches are distant, Khidmat Centres are used for cash disbursement. In some

other locations, elderly and disabled beneficiaries receive support through doorstep delivery

facilitated by ASHA workers, Anganwadi staff, and SHG volunteers.

Chhattisgarh, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh:

No special arrangements for doorstep delivery have been instituted in these states. Beneficiaries who

are unable to visit distant banks, particularly the very elderly or persons with disabilities often rely on

Common Service Centres (CSCs). At CSCs, beneficiaries’ identity is authenticated through Aadhaar after

which they withdraw their pension, typically paying a nominal service charge of around ₹10.

3.4.12 Best Practices

➢ Awareness Generation: In Jammu and Kashmir, the Department of Social Welfare undertakes

 awareness activities through print and electronic media. Additionally, Anganwadi and ASHA

 workers play an active role in disseminating information about the scheme at the community

 level. This multi-channel approach ensures that every eligible person is informed and encouraged

 to apply for the benefits.

➢ Eligibility Criteria: Instead of using the

 traditional Below Poverty Line (BPL) criteria,

 Haryana follows a Family ID system. This ID is

 issued by the government after collecting

 essential details such as the applicant’s name,

 Aadhaar number, mobile number, duration of

 residence in Haryana, and bank account

 details. In the event of a beneficiary’s death,

 the death certificate is digitally linked to the

 Family ID, ensuring automatic updates.

 Beneficiaries are automatically identified through this system and subsequently called to the block

 office for verification and disbursement. Since the process functions without interference from Gram

 Panchayat or ward officials, it has significantly reduced the scope for corruption. Beneficiaries in

 Haryana reported being highly satisfied with this transparent and efficient system, which eliminates

 the need for repeated visits to Panchayat offices. This model can be replicated by other states to

 ensure fair and smooth implementation.

➢ Disbursement of Money: In Andhra Pradesh, an efficient process is followed for pension

 disbursement. Pensions are distributed at a centrally located, easily accessible point in the village

 between the 1st and 3rd of every month. For elderly and physically challenged beneficiaries who

 are unable to visit the site, designated officials from the Panchayat Bhawan Secretariat personally

 deliver the pension amount to their homes. Similarly, in Haryana, the Social Welfare Department

 has established a partnership with banks to ensure doorstep service. If a beneficiary is unable to

 visit the bank to receive the DBT (Direct Benefit Transfer) amount due to health or mobility issues,

 they can inform the bank. The bank then takes responsibility for completing necessary formalities

 and arranging for the delivery of the amount to the beneficiary.

➢ Assistance with Application Process: During the survey, several beneficiaries mentioned facing

 difficulties in completing the application process. In Assam, local officials at the Gram Panchayat

 and Block levels assist especially vulnerable individuals, such as the elderly or persons with

 disabilities, in filling out application forms and submitting the required documents. This practice

 of extending proactive support to vulnerable groups can be adopted in other states as well to

 enhance accessibility and inclusion.

Andhra Pradesh: A Case Study

Andhra Pradesh (AP) stands out as one of the leading states in India in implementing social security pensions,

including the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP). The state has integrated NSAP within its broader NTR

Bharosa Pension Scheme, ensuring seamless service delivery, strong monitoring, and significant coverage of

vulnerable populations. The following highlights some of AP’s best practices observed during the interviews with

the state-level and district-level officials:

High State Top-Up: Andhra Pradesh provides substantially higher pension amounts as top-up over central share,

thus ensuring stronger financial security for elderly, widowed, and disabled beneficiaries.

Strong Community Involvement: SHGs, Village Organizations, and Secretariat staff play an active role in

awareness, identification, verification, and grievance support, strengthening transparency and accountability.

Support for Elderly and Disabled Beneficiaries and efficient last-mile delivery: Support is provided to elderly

and disabled beneficiaries to ensure they can access their pension amounts without difficulty. Pension

disbursement functionaries deliver pensions door-to-door, using biometric authentication to ensure accuracy and

prevent fraud. Welfare Assistants and Panchayat staff also help beneficiaries complete any necessary

documentation or verification, enabling the timely and hassle-free receipt of benefits.

Identification and Removal of Ghost Beneficiaries: Instances of bogus or ghost beneficiaries were rare but have

been identified occasionally in Andhra Pradesh. These cases are detected through cross-verification of records,

field visits, and reconciliation of beneficiary databases with Aadhaar, ration, and revenue data. Random audits

and supervisory checks at both the Mandal and District levels further assist in identifying discrepancies. Once

such cases are confirmed, the beneficiary entries are immediately removed, and recovery of any wrongly

disbursed funds is initiated. Officials responsible for lapses are held accountable, with disciplinary action and

retraining implemented to prevent recurrence.

Pension Disbursement: All Pensions including NSAP pensions are processed for payment by state own MIS

system (APT Online) and disbursed through Village/Ward Secretariat functionaries.

Social Audit: For all pension schemes of the government, including NSAP, social audit is conducted by Society for

Social Audit Accountability and Transparency (APSSAAT). This is the same unit that conducts the social audit for

MGNREGA.

Grievance Redressal Mechanisms: The state has established multiple grievance redressal mechanisms to ensure

that NSAP beneficiaries can report issues and receive timely support. Mandal Parishad Development Officers

(MPDOs) and Municipal Commissioners act as nodal officers for receiving and addressing complaints.

Beneficiaries may submit grievances through helplines, secretariat contact points, written applications at

panchayat/secretariat offices, or via phone communication. Weekly grievance redressal meetings are conducted

at the Secretariat, Mandal/Municipality, and District levels to review and resolve pending issues. Each complaint

is tracked until resolution, with corrective measures implemented to avoid repetition.

3.4.13 Convergence of NSAP with Other Schemes

Discussions with State Nodal Officials and implementing agencies indicate that the level of

convergence between NSAP and other social protection schemes varies considerably across states.

The strongest integration was observed in Bihar, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, and

Chhattisgarh, where beneficiaries are linked to multiple schemes such as PDS, PMAY-G/urban housing,

employment under MGNREGA, and NRLM/NULM SHGs. Bihar and Jammu Kashmir demonstrates

near-universal convergence with all the schemes, including coverage under the Aam Aadmi Bima

Yojana (AABY) and priority access for beneficiaries or their children under the Ajeevika Skills

Programme. In contrast, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh exhibit limited convergence, primarily with PDS

and MGNREGA. Officials in these two states noted that responsibility for other schemes lies with other

departments, leading to limited awareness of convergence with other schemes. Tamil Nadu, Andhra

Pradesh and Telangana further strengthen convergence through robust integration with state health

insurance schemes. Overall, while food security and MGNREGA linkages remain consistently strong,

convergence with insurance and skill development programmes is uneven and continues to depend

on state-level coordination and data-sharing mechanisms.

As outlined in the guidelines, special efforts are required to ensure that eligible NSAP beneficiaries

receive housing support under Indira Awaas Yojana and related urban housing schemes. In this

context, Gram Panchayats and ward officials were asked whether any households in their area had

been given priority under the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY). A significant 72% confirmed that

such priority allotments had been made. Among the categories prioritized, widow-headed households

received the highest preference (90.1%), followed by households with persons with disabilities

(84.3%). However, households with elderly members were reported to be considerably less

prioritized. These findings suggest that while PMAY is successfully targeting key vulnerable groups,

additional efforts may be needed to ensure more equitable inclusion of elderly.

3.4.14 Other State-specific Pension Schemes

The impact of the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) needs to be viewed in the context of

the wider social protection framework at the state level. In addition to the centrally sponsored NSAP,

beneficiary groups like the elderly, widows, and persons with disabilities. These state schemes operate

independently of NSAP, though their objectives and design substantially overlap. For instance, Bihar

implements the State Disability Pension scheme; Chhattisgarh runs the CM Pension Scheme; while

Andhra Pradesh and Telangana have consolidated assistance under the NTR Bharosa and Asara

programmes respectively. The coexistence of these programmes highlights the federal diversity in

social protection delivery. While this demonstrates strong state commitment to supporting vulnerable

populations, it also raises important considerations regarding duplication, targeting efficiency, and

data integration between state and central schemes.

4.1.1 Income and Source of Livelihood

The transition from joint to nuclear family structures has significant implications for old-age income

security. Analysis of household size data indicates that in several states such as Andhra Pradesh,

Haryana, Tamil Nadu, and Telangana, the average family size is as low as two members, suggesting a

higher likelihood of elderly persons living alone or with only one other dependent. In contrast, states

such as Assam, Bihar, Chhattisgarh, and Uttar Pradesh show an average family size of around three

members, while Gujarat and Jammu & Kashmir report relatively larger households with an average size

of four members. In states with smaller household sizes, the diminishing role of traditional family-based

support systems increases the vulnerability of the elderly, underscoring the importance of social pensions

as a critical and often primary source of income support.

The study findings (Table 4.1) reflect this trend in household livelihood patterns, wherein social

assistance and pensions constitute the primary source of income for nearly 24% of households.

Dependence on these transfers is substantially higher in states such as Andhra Pradesh and Telangana,

where more than half of households report social assistance or pensions as their main source of income.

In Chhattisgarh, 38.3% of households identify pensions or social assistance as a primary income source,

underscoring their critical role in supporting vulnerable populations. At the same time, a significant

proportion of households continue to rely on wage labour and small-scale farming.

5.2 Gaps in Achievement of Outcomes

While the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) continues to play a crucial role in providing

basic social security, several gaps remain in achieving its intended outcomes of ensuring income

security, dignity, and inclusion for the elderly, widows, and persons with disabilities.

• Inadequate Pension Amounts:

 The outcome of improved livelihood security has been only partially achieved due to the low

 central pension contribution (Rs.200-Rs.500 per month), which has not been revised since 2012.

 Beneficiary surveys revealed that only 6% of respondents considered the pension amount fully

 adequate, while the majority found it insufficient to meet essential needs such as food and medical

 expenses. Nearly 90% of beneficiaries expressed that the financial assistance should be increased.

 Several respondents expressed a demand for an increase in the monthly assistance to at least

 Rs.3,000 to maintain a minimum standard of living.

• Exclusion Errors in Beneficiary Identification:

 The continued reliance on outdated BPL lists has resulted in the exclusion of many deserving elderly

 persons, widows, and persons with disabilities, particularly in Chhattisgarh, Assam, and Bihar. Field

findings also indicated that households without BPL cards or Aadhaar-linked bank accounts are often

 left out of the scheme, thereby undermining its inclusiveness and intended coverage.

• Irregular Payments and Accessibility Challenges:

 Although the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) system has improved the timeliness and transparency

 of pension disbursements in several states, operational challenges persist due to delays in KYC

6 CHAPTER

 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

6.1 CONCLUSION

The National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) is a key social welfare scheme of the Government

of India that provides basic financial support to vulnerable groups such as the elderly, widows, and

persons with disabilities. The evaluation study conducted across the ten states of Assam, Andhra

Pradesh, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana, Gujarat, Jammu & Kashmir, Tamil Nadu, and

Chhattisgarh revealed that the scheme has made significant progress in ensuring social inclusion and

promoting a sense of dignity among its beneficiaries.

The Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) mechanism of disbursement of pension has enhanced

transparency, minimized leakages, and improved accountability in fund flow. In several states, the

system functions smoothly, with timely crediting of pension amounts and high levels of satisfaction

among beneficiaries. However, the study also highlighted persistent gaps in awareness, coverage,

and efficiency. Beneficiary awareness of scheme provisions, eligibility criteria, and responsible

departments remains uneven across states. In many cases, beneficiaries are unaware of whether

the funds are contributed by the central or state government. The identification process also varies

across states. While some states adopt community-driven approaches of identification through

Gram Sabha or panchayat officials, others apply themselves or rely on official verification,

sometimes leading to inconsistencies in beneficiary inclusion and exclusion.

The evaluation study revealed variations in the ease of accessing benefits. While DBT has been

widely implemented, a substantial proportion of beneficiaries, particularly in remote, or inaccessible

areas, still face challenges such as long travel distances to banks, biometric failures, and delayed

credits. States like Andhra Pradesh, which have adopted door-to-door or village-level disbursement

systems, demonstrate better accessibility and beneficiary satisfaction. The adequacy of financial

assistance under NSAP is another concern raised consistently during field interactions. Although the

pension provides critical support, the current amount is insufficient to meet even the basic needs of

beneficiaries, especially considering inflation and rising healthcare costs. Several states provide a

top up over the central share with their own funds, but the level of enhancement differs widely,

leading to disparities in the level of support provided across states.

--- PAGE 95 ---
The study also found that social audits and grievance redressal mechanisms, though present in the

guidelines, are inconsistently implemented. Many local officials either lack awareness of the process

or the capacity to conduct regular audits. Despite these challenges, NSAP has contributed

meaningfully to the social and economic stability of vulnerable households. Beneficiaries reported

that pensions help them manage essential household expenses, purchase medicines, and reduce

dependence on others, thereby improving their sense of dignity and autonomy. The scheme,

therefore, continues to play a crucial role in reducing vulnerability to income and health shocks,

especially for the elderly, widowed, and disabled populations.

6.2 CHALLENGES

While the scheme has provided critical support to vulnerable populations, systemic and operational

issues persist at various stages from beneficiary identification to disbursement and grievance

resolution. Some of the key challenges identified during the survey have been presented below:

1. Identification and Exclusion Errors

The requirement of being listed in the Below Poverty Line (BPL) database for eligibility continues to

exclude many deserving individuals. In Chhattisgarh, Assam, and Bihar, respondents mentioned that

several elderly, widowed, or disabled persons were denied pensions because their names were

missing from the outdated BPL lists or due to lack of required documentation such as Aadhaar, caste,

or medical certificates. In Uttar Pradesh, field observations indicated irregularities at the beneficiary

identification level, where Gram Panchayats play a key role in issuing income certificates. Instances

of inclusion of ineligible beneficiaries and exclusion of deserving ones were reported, pointing to the

need for stronger oversight and data verification mechanisms.

2. Documentation and Administrative Delays

In Jammu & Kashmir and Bihar, the process of submitting and verifying supporting documents (e.g.,

Date of Birth, Disability Certificates, and Income Certificates) often leads to long delays. Frequent

internet disruptions and limited digital capacity at local offices further slowdown application updates

on the online portal. Beneficiaries are required to make repeated visits to government offices,

incurring travel costs and time.

3. Bank and Disbursement-Related Challenges

In several states, KYC related issues have caused interruptions in pension payments. In Gujarat and

Chhattisgarh, beneficiaries reported that pension disbursements were stopped if their bank KYC was

incomplete, and they were not adequately informed about the process or timeline for rectification. In

rural and hilly areas of Chhattisgarh and Jammu & Kashmir, the distance to the nearest bank branch was

cited as a significant hardship, especially for elderly women who had to pay for transportation or rely on

others to collect the pension. In Assam and Telangana, disbursement delays were reported due to

irregular fund flow and technical issues in Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) systems, including Aadhaar-

--- PAGE 96 ---
linking errors, inactive bank accounts, and poor internet connectivity in remote areas.

4. Challenges in Biometric Authentication and Disbursement Agents

In Tamil Nadu, beneficiaries particularly the elderly and persons with disabilities face difficulties

during biometric authentication due to worn fingerprints or health conditions. Many spend several

hours at banks, post offices, or Customer Service Centres (CSCs) to successfully withdraw their

pension. Reports also indicate that in some cases, disbursing agents such as postmen or CSC

operators deduct small commissions or service charges (ranging from ₹10 to ₹30 per transaction),

and in few instances, partial or full withdrawal of pension without beneficiary consent was reported.

5. Lack of Awareness and Information Gaps

In Assam and Bihar, awareness about eligibility criteria, documentation requirements, and scheme

procedures remains limited. Beneficiaries often rely on informal information channels and are

unaware of grievance redressal mechanisms. This lack of formal awareness and outreach has

resulted in low participation among the most vulnerable sections of the population.

6.3 LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

Certain limitations were encountered while conducting the study:

 ➢ Difficulty in Collecting Responses from Very Elderly Beneficiaries:

 It was challenging to collect detailed responses from beneficiaries aged 80 years and above,

 particularly under the Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme (IGNOAPS). Many

 respondents had difficulty recalling specific details related to scheme implementation,

 payment frequency, or grievance redressal. To ensure accuracy, assistance from family

 members or caregivers was sought during interviews.

 ➢ Caste Representation and Sampling Gaps:

 Although efforts were made to maintain the caste-wise quota as per the planned sample,

 achieving the desired distribution was difficult. In several Gram Panchayats, one or more caste

 categories were entirely absent, especially in smaller or homogeneous villages. The team

 attempted to compensate for these gaps by covering the missing caste groups at the district or

 state level, but it was not always possible to meet the pre-decided quota exactly as designed.

 ➢ Limited Awareness and Identification of NFBS Beneficiaries:

 Identifying beneficiaries under the National Family Benefit Scheme (NFBS) proved particularly

 difficult due to low awareness and limited record maintenance at the local level. In many

 cases, Gram Panchayat and ward officials were consulted to help locate NFBS households;

 however, even local authorities were sometimes unaware of existing beneficiaries. This

indicates a broader issue of low visibility and awareness of the NFBS component among both

 communities and local functionaries.

 ➢ Reliance on Local Officials and Family Assistance:

 In certain cases, field investigators had to depend on support from Panchayat representatives

 or family members to locate and verify beneficiaries, especially in remote or scattered

 habitations.

6.4 RECOMMENDATIONS

Presented hereunder are some recommendations which can help eliminate systemic barriers and

further strengthen the scheme implementation process:

6.4.1 Justification and Recommendation for Continuation of the Scheme

Introducing a Minimum Floor-level Pension

The Government of India introduced NSAP in 1995. In 2012, the monthly pension amount was revised

to Rs. 200 for old age people between 60 and 79 years of age, to Rs. 300 for widows (40-79 years) and

persons with disability (18-79 years), and Rs. 500 for beneficiaries of 80 years of age and above. Since

then, there has been no upward revision in this amount although consumption expenditure has grown

substantially over the years and in the context of India making rapid strides on economic front and

recently becoming the 4th largest economy of the world. In this background, an attempt has been

made in the present section to develop an argument for revising the floor level of social assistant

benefits under NSAP to restore their adequacy, coverage and constitutional relevance.

It is therefore recommended to introduce a National Floor Pension (NFP) amount, similar to the

National Floor Level Minimum Wage set by the Ministry of Labour and Employment. This would ensure

a minimum level of financial assistance uniformly across all states. The NFP should be linked to the

Consumer Price Index (CPI) to automatically adjust with inflation, with annual revisions based on the

cost of living. Additionally, states should be encouraged to provide top-ups to the central share in a

uniform and transparent manner to reduce regional disparities in benefit levels.

on CPI, for the NFBS component under NSAP, the assistance amount of ₹20,000 should have increased

to Rs. 38,200, which, if rounded, would approximate Rs. 40,000.

6.4.3 Recommendation on Reforming NSAP Disability Pension Eligibility

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 defines a “person with benchmark disability” as

someone with not less than 40% disability certified by a competent authority. This definition applies

across legal entitlements, including reservations, concessions, and welfare benefits. Under current

guidelines, only individuals with 80% or more disability qualify for the IGNDPS pension. This creates a

misalignment between the pension eligibility criteria and the statutory definition of disability in the

RPwD Act. The existing 80% threshold inherently excludes many people who face significant functional

limitations, economic hardship, and social barriers but have disability percentages between 40% and

79%. Research indicates that this criterion contributes to systematic exclusion of a large portion of the
disabled population that otherwise meet legal thresholds for disability recognition.21 Recent judicial

interventions have criticized arbitrary disability thresholds in policy contexts when they depart from

the statutory benchmark of 40%. For example, the Punjab and Haryana High Court held that state

policies privileging only higher disability percentages are inconsistent with the RPwD Act’s benchmark
definition and discriminatory.22

Thus, amending the NSAP to align the IGNDPS eligibility with the 40% benchmark disability threshold

will reduce exclusion, enhance equity, improve administrative coherence, and uphold legal standards

established by the RPwD Act, 2016.

6.4.4 Recommendations on Revision of Beneficiary Cap

The current central beneficiary caps under the National Social Assistance Programme are largely based

on population estimates from the early 2010s and do not adequately reflect recent demographic

changes, particularly population ageing, increased widowhood, and rising longevity among persons

with disabilities. Since the fixation of these caps, India has experienced substantial demographic

changes which have direct implications for the potential beneficiary base of NSAP Pension schemes.

protection floor, leading to uneven coverage across states and beneficiary categories. It is worthwhile

mentioning here that the NSAP Task Force, constituted by the MoRD in 2013 recommended expansion
of beneficiary coverage in a phased manner.24

In view of the projected increase in vulnerable populations by 2030 and the estimated coverage

requirements based on a 20 per cent poverty rate, the continuation of static central beneficiary caps

risks diluting the effectiveness of NSAP. It is therefore recommended that the central beneficiary caps

under the Indira Gandhi National Widow Pension Scheme (IGNWPS) and the Indira Gandhi National

Disability Pension Scheme (IGNDPS) be revised upward by at least 15-20 per cent, in line with the

projected growth in the respective target populations. Further, given the substantially higher increase

in the elderly population, the central beneficiary cap under the Indira Gandhi National Old Age

Pension Scheme (IGNOAPS) may be increased by at least 25 per cent, to ensure alignment with the

estimated coverage requirement by 2030. Further, the beneficiary caps under NSAP may be indexed

to demographic projections and revised once every five years, based on Census data or official

population projections by the Registrar General of India, and growth trends in elderly, widow, and

disability populations.

A timely upward revision of caps, along with an institutional mechanism for periodic updates, is

essential to ensure that NSAP continues to provide minimum income security, dignity, and social

protection to the most vulnerable sections of the population.

6.4.5 Recommendations on Improving Scheme Implementation

Beneficiary Identification

Evidence from the field indicates that many eligible elderly persons, widows, and persons with

disabilities were excluded from the scheme because the targeting process relied heavily on outdated

BPL lists. To address this, a robust verification system for beneficiary identification should be

established. The existing BPL list may be retained as a preliminary reference but should be

supplemented with a simple vulnerability assessment form at the Gram Panchayat level. This form

should capture key socio-economic details such as caste, income, household dependents, land

ownership, widowhood, and disability status. The collected data can then be validated using Social

Registries or other integrated beneficiary databases (such as Family ID databases), where available,

to accurately ascertain applicants’ eligibility for the scheme.

Gram Panchayats may conduct annual verification drives during Gram Sabha meetings, displaying

updated lists publicly for community validation. States such as Haryana, which effectively uses its

 92

--- PAGE 106 ---
Family ID system, can serve as reference models. State governments should be entrusted with the

responsibility to define its own inclusion criteria based on local characteristics and administrative

capacity, while ensuring transparency and accountability. The Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD)

may provide a standard verification framework and inclusion template for reference, allowing states

the flexibility to customize it for their implementation context.

Streamlining Application, Verification, and Appeal Process

The study observed significant variation across states in how applications are received, verified, and

sanctioned. To bring consistency and accountability, uniform Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)

should be developed by MoRD and adopted by all states. The SOPs should clearly define timelines

and responsibilities for:

 ➢ Application receipt (both online and offline, in local language)

 ➢ Document verification

 ➢ Sanction or rejection

 ➢ Appeal and review mechanism

 ➢ Annual re-verification of beneficiaries

These SOPs should be translated into regional languages, disseminated to all Gram Panchayats, and

integrated with NSAP-PPS for time-stamped tracking of each stage. Training modules for Panchayat

Secretaries and SHG representatives can be developed to ensure effective implementation.

Ensuring Timely and Accessible Pension Payments

Although Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) has improved pension delivery, delays and biometric

authentication failures still hinder timely payments, especially in rural and hilly regions such as Jammu

and Kashmir. Beneficiaries, particularly the elderly and persons with disabilities often face difficulties

traveling to banks or Common Service Centres (CSCs) for withdrawals. To address these challenges:

 ➢ Alternative authentication methods such as OTP or PIN-based verification should be enabled

 in case of biometric or connectivity issues.

 ➢ For beneficiaries above 80 years of age or with severe disabilities, states should

 institutionalize doorstep pension delivery models, drawing on best practices from Haryana

 (bank tie-ups for home visits) and Andhra Pradesh (village-level pension disbursement day).

 ➢ Any delay beyond the fixed disbursement date should trigger an automatic alert to the Block

 Nodal Officer, who must ensure resolution within 15 days.

MoRD may issue an advisory directing banks and state nodal agencies to certify Business

Correspondents (BCs) for home delivery in such cases.

Strengthening the Social Audit Process

Despite being mandated, social audits under NSAP are not regularly conducted in most Gram

Panchayats. To enhance transparency and community oversight, states should institutionalize the

annual social audits for NSAP at the Gram Panchayat level with participation from community

members, SHGs, and ward representatives.

A digital audit checklist may be used to simplify the process and standardize reporting. An Action

Taken Report (ATR) must be prepared and publicly displayed within 30 days of each audit. MoRD may

develop a mobile-based data entry form for uploading audit findings to NSAP-PPS.

Revamping the Grievance Redressal Mechanism

Grievance redressal systems vary widely across states and are often poorly documented. Many

beneficiaries are still unaware of where or how to lodge complaints, and there is limited monitoring

of resolution timelines.

To improve accountability, a three-tier Grievance Redressal Mechanism should be established at the

Gram Panchayat, District, and State levels, fully integrated with NSAP-PPS. Beneficiaries should be

able to submit complaints through multiple channels like Gram Sabha register, toll-free helpline,

SMS, or WhatsApp and receive an auto-generated acknowledgment number along with periodic

status updates.

Unresolved cases at the Panchayat level should automatically escalate to the district office within 15

days and to the state social welfare department after 30 days. Quarterly analysis of grievance trends

should be conducted to identify recurring issues such as payment delays or bank-related problems

and take corrective actions accordingly.

Monitoring and Evaluation

Although NSAP-PPS provides comprehensive data, it remains underutilized for real-time decision-

making or policy feedback. There is a need to shift from administrative reporting to data-driven

monitoring and evidence-based evaluation. A dashboard-based monitoring system should be

institutionalized at the state and district levels to track coverage, payment timeliness, grievance

resolution, and audit completion rates. Existing MIS platforms can be enhanced or integrated with

NSAP-PPS to ensure efficiency. Additionally, independent third-party evaluations should be

conducted every three years to assess scheme performance, inclusion, and beneficiary satisfaction.

Performance indicators should be linked to annual state-level review meetings to promote

accountability and continuous improvement.

6.4.6 Other Recommendations

Local Monitoring

At the District and Gram Panchayat level, strong local monitoring and facilitation mechanisms should be

devised for ensuring transparency, accuracy, and last-mile efficiency in NSAP implementation. Each GP

should maintain an updated live register of beneficiaries, to ensure real-time tracking of active

beneficiaries and easy public access. To maintain the integrity of beneficiary data, annual verification and

enrolment camps should be organized for inclusion of newly eligible individuals and removal of ineligible

or deceased beneficiaries. Transparency can be further enhanced by publicly displaying fund release and

disbursement details on GP notice boards and discussing them during Panchayat meetings.

Strengthening last-mile delivery

To further strengthen last-mile support, beneficiary facilitation centres at the village or ward level

could be established which would serve as a single-point interface between beneficiaries and social

security service providers. These centres can assist with registration, application form filling,

verification of applications, appeal, grievance resolution, and regular updates on scheme

entitlements. Local volunteers or members of SHGs, ASHA, or elected PRI representatives could be

engaged to assist the elderly, widowed, and disabled beneficiaries navigate documentation and

banking processes. Periodic capacity-building of these volunteers by district officials should be

conducted to increase their effectiveness.

In Haryana, the Social Welfare Department has established a successful system in collaboration with

banks, wherein if a beneficiary is too old, disabled or suffering from medical issues, they can inform

the bank, which then completes the necessary formalities and sends a representative to disburse the

pension amount at the beneficiary’s residence. This initiative ensures that the most vulnerable

recipients receive their benefits without any hardship or dependence on others. Similar mechanisms

for doorstep or assisted benefit delivery can be adopted by other states to strengthen last-mile

access, particularly for elderly and differently-abled beneficiaries who face challenges in visiting

banks or payment centres.

Convergence with other schemes

Establishing convergence with other welfare schemes can improve the procedural efficiencies at state

level. Many states run parallel welfare programs for elderly, widow and disabled. Establishing data-

sharing or referral mechanisms among the divisions catering to similar target groups may help expand

the outreach of the programmes among eligible beneficiaries. Also, a mechanism may be put in place

where non-eligible people would be directed to other appropriate state scheme (like Cheyutha

Pension Scheme in Telangana or Laxmibai Samajik Suraksha Pension Yojana in Bihar). The beneficiaries

can also be linked with other complementary schemes such as PM Awas Yojana, NFSA, Health

Insurance schemes and other livelihood initiatives to enhance social and economic resilience.

Such convergence may help prevent overlaps and leverage existing outreach networks. At the State

level, a Convergence Committee could be set up consisting of Heads of various departments such as

Health, Social Justice, Social Welfare, Rural Development etc. in order to devise unique ways of

convergence.
